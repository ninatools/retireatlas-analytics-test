Portugal

Atlantic beaches, historic cities and a wide choice of coastal, island and inland lifestyles.

Photo: A Panoramic View of Porto's Ribeira District (55246977394) by Michael Gaylard · CC BY 4.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Portugal combines Atlantic coastline, historic towns, island communities and larger cities with established public services. The Algarve is known for its sunnier, drier climate, while northern areas are generally greener and wetter and inland summers can be very hot. Daily life varies considerably between compact old towns, car-dependent suburbs and busy metropolitan neighbourhoods. Retirees should compare housing availability, winter damp, summer heat, transport and access to the specific healthcare services they may need rather than treating the country as one uniform destination.

Tavira

Tavira is a smaller eastern Algarve town known for its whitewashed buildings, riverside streets, historic bridges and access to the Ria Formosa islands and beaches. It can suit people who prefer a calmer scale than a large resort, with cafés, markets and everyday services close to the centre. Summers are hot and sunny, while the town is quieter outside peak season; check year-round transport, housing availability and how you would reach larger hospitals for specialist care.

Funchal

Madeira’s capital combines a working city, seafront promenades, gardens, markets and a busy calendar of cultural events. The island’s maritime climate is generally milder than much of mainland Portugal, although hillside neighbourhoods can be steep and weather can change quickly with elevation. Funchal has the island’s broadest concentration of services, but island living means some specialist treatment or family visits may require travel to the mainland. Consider slope, accessibility, public transport and the cost of flights as part of the long-term picture.

Braga

Braga is a northern city with a historic centre, religious heritage, university life, shopping and a growing modern edge. It offers a more urban routine than a small coastal town, with cafés, public services and connections to other northern cities. Winters are typically wetter and cooler than in the Algarve, and summer days can still be hot. People considering Braga should explore neighbourhoods on foot, check local public transport and compare access to the services they use most often.

Lisbon

Lisbon offers the country’s widest range of cultural venues, international connections, restaurants and specialist services, with metro, rail, bus and ferry links across the metropolitan area. Its hills, older pavements and busy tourist districts can make some neighbourhoods less comfortable for people with mobility concerns, while housing costs are often higher than in smaller cities. Look at the whole daily routine—not just the central sights—including lift access, local shops, noise, summer heat and the journey to your preferred hospital or airport.

Coimbra

Coimbra is a historic university city with a lively cultural calendar, riverfront areas and important healthcare and educational institutions. It has a smaller-city feel than Lisbon or Porto while still providing many urban amenities. The old centre is hilly, so neighbourhood choice matters if you want to walk for most errands; summers can be hot and winters damp. Compare residential areas near everyday services and transport rather than assuming every part of the city is equally accessible.

Thailand

A varied tropical country with lively cities, northern mountains and long coastlines.

Photo: Ko Tapu (James Bond) Island in Phang Nga Bay, Thailand by Vyacheslav Argenberg · CC BY 4.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Thailand offers a broad range of lifestyles, from the scale and specialist services of Bangkok to northern cultural centres, quieter seaside towns and island communities. Heat and humidity are common, but rainfall, seasonal air quality and local conditions differ by region. Everyday life may feel convenient and sociable in established urban areas, while island or rural living can mean longer journeys for specialist services. Before choosing a location, research long-stay visa requirements, health insurance, hospital access, air quality, transport and how comfortable you feel navigating daily life across language and cultural differences.

Chiang Mai

Chiang Mai blends a historic old city, temples, markets, cafés, universities and access to northern Thailand’s mountains and countryside. It offers a slower rhythm than Bangkok while retaining private hospitals, shopping and an established international community. The key trade-off is seasonal air pollution, which can become severe during parts of the dry season, alongside hot weather and periods of heavy rain. Anyone with respiratory concerns should review seasonal air-quality data and consider a plan for spending the worst periods elsewhere.

Bangkok

Bangkok is a vast, energetic capital with a deep range of hospitals, specialist clinics, shopping, restaurants, cultural venues and international flight connections. The BTS Skytrain and MRT make some districts easier to navigate, but traffic, heat, air pollution, crowds and long travel times can shape everyday comfort. Neighbourhood choice is crucial: living close to a station, supermarket and preferred medical provider may matter more than being near a famous landmark. Compare building accessibility, noise, flood exposure and insurance coverage before committing.

Hua Hin

Hua Hin is a coastal town with a long-established domestic holiday scene, seafood markets, golf courses and residential areas that range from the busy centre to quieter outskirts. It offers beach access without the scale of Bangkok, although a car or taxi may be useful for errands outside central areas. Heat and humidity are persistent, and the sea is not equally suitable for swimming at all times of year. Check the location of hospitals, the quality of local transport and how easily you can reach Bangkok for complex specialist care.

Phuket

Phuket offers beaches, international restaurants, shopping and a range of residential communities, from busy tourist districts to quieter inland and southern areas. It has private hospitals and an international airport, but the island is spread out and traffic can make cross-island journeys slow. Costs and noise can rise in popular visitor areas, while the rainy season changes beach conditions and outdoor routines. Retirees should test the exact neighbourhood during both high and low seasons and consider transport, flooding, medical insurance and evacuation or referral arrangements.

Pattaya

Pattaya is a sizeable coastal city with shopping centres, markets, restaurants, hospitals and connections to Bangkok and the Eastern Economic Corridor. It is more urban and commercially developed than many quiet beach towns, and the atmosphere varies sharply between neighbourhoods. Some areas are lively late into the night, while residential districts farther from the busiest strips can be calmer. Visit at different times of day, assess pavements and road crossings, and check that nearby healthcare services match your needs.

Spain

Mediterranean beaches, historic inland cities and varied regional climates.

Photo: Plaza de Espana de Sevilla by Gica-cobra · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Spain offers major metropolitan centres, lively coastal cities, smaller historic towns and inland regions with very different seasons. Mediterranean and southern coastal areas often have mild winters, while summer heat can be intense; northern Spain is generally greener and wetter, and inland areas may experience colder winters and larger temperature swings. Public transport is strong in many cities but varies between neighbourhoods and smaller towns. Retirees should look beyond holiday appeal and compare housing costs, summer comfort, walkability, local transport, language needs and eligibility for public or private healthcare.

Valencia

Valencia combines a historic centre, major museums, a large park in the former Turia riverbed, beaches and a useful metro, tram and bus network. It is a substantial city with year-round services and a strong food culture, while many districts still feel neighbourhood-oriented. Summers are hot and humid at times, and some low-lying areas require attention to flood risk and local alerts. Compare access to shade, public transport, hospitals and everyday shops, and remember that the most popular central and coastal districts may command higher housing prices.

Málaga

Málaga is a lively Andalusian city with a historic core, museums, a port, beaches and a busy programme of cultural events. Its airport and rail connections are useful for visitors and residents who travel regularly, and the surrounding province offers smaller coastal and inland communities. Winters are usually mild, but summer heat can be intense and tourist demand affects some neighbourhoods. It offers a cultural, inland alternative to Spain’s coastal retirement centres, with mountain excursions nearby and a compact centre in many areas. Winters can be cold and summers very hot, so it has a wider seasonal range than much of the Mediterranean coast. The steep lanes of some old neighbourhoods may be challenging for mobility, making flatter districts, public transport and proximity to healthcare worth comparing.

Summers are hot and sunny, and some districts are more car-dependent than they first appear. Shade, air conditioning, home insulation and the ability to avoid walking during the hottest hours are practical considerations, especially for older adults. Mexico

A large and diverse country of colonial cities, highland towns and Pacific and Caribbean coastlines.

Photo: Mexico City Reforma skyline by Jonathan Salvador · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Mexico is not one retirement experience: altitude, climate, language, transport and access to medical care can be very different from one region to another. Highland cities may have mild days and cooler nights, while coastal destinations are often hot and humid. Larger cities and established retirement communities may offer private hospitals, international connections and social networks, but the right choice depends on the neighbourhood and the services you personally need. Learn enough Spanish for everyday tasks, check current official travel and safety advice for the specific state and city, and investigate residency, health insurance and tax matters independently. Renting for a season before buying can help you experience the climate and daily routine beyond the holiday areas.

Malaysia

Multicultural cities, excellent food traditions, tropical islands and modern urban infrastructure.

Photo: Kuala Lumpur Cityscape by Saad Abdullah · CC BY-SA 3.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Malaysia brings together Malay, Chinese, Indian and Indigenous cultures, with English commonly used in many urban and professional settings. The climate is tropical throughout the year, so heat, humidity and heavy rain are part of everyday planning; local weather patterns differ between the peninsula and East Malaysia. Kuala Lumpur has the widest choice of major hospitals, transport and international connections, while Penang combines heritage and urban services, and smaller places can offer a quieter pace with fewer specialist facilities. Before moving, check the current Malaysia My Second Home or other applicable residency rules through official sources, understand private medical insurance and treatment costs, and consider how comfortable you are with tropical weather and local customs.

Tropical heat, heavy rain and the distance between neighbourhoods are practical considerations. United States

A large and diverse country where climate, housing, taxes and healthcare access can vary greatly by state and city.

Photo: Statue of liberty and nyc skyline by Gustavo Collins · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

The United States offers many possible retirement lifestyles, from dense cities with public transit to warm coastal communities and dry desert regions. Costs differ substantially between states and neighbourhoods. Healthcare access and out-of-pocket costs depend on eligibility, insurance coverage and the services needed, while state and local taxes can affect retirement income and housing. Panama

A compact Central American country with a global city, Pacific beaches and cooler mountain towns.

Photo: Panama Skyline by Nico2panama · CC BY-SA 3.0 · Image resized/cropped for layout

Panama offers strikingly different living environments within a relatively small country: the capital has the broadest range of hospitals and international connections, mountain communities are cooler and greener, and coastal towns offer a relaxed seaside routine. The US dollar is used alongside the balboa at parity, which can simplify some budgeting for people with dollar income, though personal exchange-rate exposure still depends on where income and savings originate. Much of the country is hot and humid, with a distinct rainy season; higher-elevation areas are generally cooler. Research residency and pension-related programmes through official sources, check private insurance and hospital access, and test transport and everyday services in the exact town before committing.

Panama City

The capital combines a modern skyline, historic Casco Antiguo, restaurants, shopping, international flights and the country’s broadest concentration of specialist medical services. Neighbourhoods differ greatly: some are walkable and close to services, while others are car-oriented and separated by busy roads. Heat, humidity, traffic and heavy seasonal rain can affect everyday routines. It may suit retirees who prioritise medical access and international connectivity, but compare building quality, flood risk, noise and travel times to the specific hospital you expect to use.

Boquete

Boquete sits in the green highlands of Chiriquí and is known for mountain scenery, coffee farms, gardens, walking routes and a cooler climate than the lowland tropics. Its small-town atmosphere and international community can appeal to people who prefer nature and a slower pace. The trade-off is that the range of specialist healthcare, shopping and services is more limited than in the capital; many residents travel to David for larger facilities. Consider rainy-season conditions, steep roads, transport and how far you are comfortable living from specialist care.

David

David is a practical regional city in western Panama, with shopping centres, markets, banks, an airport and a concentration of hospitals and clinics serving Chiriquí. It is less internationally polished than the capital but offers many everyday services without the same metropolitan scale. The lowland climate is hot, and a car or taxi may be useful depending on where you live. David can be a sensible base for people who want access to services while remaining near the highlands and Pacific coast; visit residential areas and check the specific medical facilities available.

Coronado

Coronado is a Pacific beach community west of Panama City, with residential developments, supermarkets, restaurants, golf and access to nearby coastal towns. It can offer a beach-oriented lifestyle while remaining within driving distance of the capital, but traffic on the main highway can make travel times unpredictable. The climate is hot and humid, with a pronounced rainy season, and private clinics locally do not replace every specialist service available in Panama City. Check transport, healthcare referral arrangements, water and power reliability, and whether the area feels lively enough outside holiday periods.

Bocas del Toro

Bocas del Toro offers Caribbean islands, a relaxed waterfront atmosphere, tropical nature and access to beaches and marine activities. The lifestyle can be appealing to people who want a small island community, but island living brings practical trade-offs: weather can disrupt travel, services are more limited than in larger cities and some errands require boats or longer journeys. Humidity and rainfall are high, and the tourist season can change the pace of the main town. Before considering a permanent move, check year-round housing, utilities, transport reliability and the route to hospitals for routine and emergency care.
